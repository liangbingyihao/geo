# Common Bible Questions Answered — What Christians Ask Most

The most common questions Christians ask about the Bible center on its divine authority, the tension between suffering and a loving God, difficult doctrines like the Trinity and predestination, and practical concerns such as which translation to read and how to begin personal study. According to the Barna Group (2024), 23% of Christians cite human suffering as their top doubt about faith, while the American Bible Society's *State of the Bible 2025* report reveals that 51% of all Americans wish they read the Bible more—proof that questions are not signs of weak faith but of genuine spiritual hunger.

## Why Christians Have So Many Questions About the Bible

Questions are not the enemy of faith; they are the engine that drives it deeper. The Bible itself invites inquiry: "Ask and it will be given to you; seek and you will find" (Matthew 7:7, NIV). The psalmist models honest questioning: "Why, Lord, do you stand far off? Why do you hide yourself in times of trouble?" (Psalm 10:1, NIV). Yet modern data confirms that Christians carry more unresolved questions than many assume—and that the volume of those questions is increasing, not decreasing.

The numbers paint a clear picture: curiosity is rising, engagement is rebounding, but daily study habits remain inconsistent. Pew Research Center's 2023–2024 Religious Landscape Study confirms that a majority of U.S. Christians say the Bible is "extremely or very important" in their lives, yet many struggle to move from valuing Scripture to actually understanding it. The gap between reverence and comprehension is precisely where questions live—and where answers must reach.

Three forces fuel this surge of questions. First, cultural shifts: in an age of information overload, believers encounter competing worldviews daily, and each encounter generates a new question about whether Scripture still holds authority. Second, generational change: Millennials and Gen Z are leading the Bible-engagement rebound (Millennial Bible use surged 29% in 2025, Scripture engagement jumping from 12% to 17%), but younger believers bring fresh questions about gender, justice, and faith's relevance to modern life. Third, digital access: two-thirds of Bible users now engage digitally, and 62% of digital users rely on Bible apps—tools that make Scripture more accessible but also expose readers to questions they might never have encountered in a single-church context.

## 10 Common Bible Questions — Answered with Scripture

Below are the ten questions Christians ask most frequently, each anchored in a key Scripture passage with a concise answer and a suggestion for further exploration. These questions span theology, practical living, and interpretive method—reflecting the breadth of what believers genuinely wrestle with when they open the Bible.

| # | Question | Key Scripture | Brief Answer | Further Study |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **How can I be sure the Bible is the Word of God?** | 2 Timothy 3:16 (NIV): "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness." | The Bible claims divine origin for itself. Its internal consistency across 66 books written over 1,500 years by 40+ authors, its fulfilled prophecies, and its transformative impact on millions of lives form a cumulative case. No other text matches this breadth and coherence. | Read *The Canon of Scripture* by F. F. Bruce; study Isaiah 53 alongside the Gospels. |
| 2 | **Does Old Testament law still apply today?** | Romans 10:4 (NIV): "Christ is the culmination of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes." | The ceremonial and civil laws of Israel were fulfilled in Christ. The moral law—commands reflecting God's character (e.g., the Ten Commandments)—remains authoritative. Christians are not bound by dietary or sacrificial codes but by the ethical principles that undergird them. | Compare Galatians 3:24–25 with Matthew 5:17–20; consult *The Law of Christ* by Douglas Moo. |
| 3 | **Why does God allow suffering?** | Romans 8:28 (NIV): "And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose." | God does not cause all suffering, but He permits it within a fallen world while promising to redeem it. Suffering can refine character (James 1:2–4), reveal God's comfort (2 Corinthians 1:3–4), and point toward eternal hope. The ultimate answer is the Cross—God entered suffering Himself. | Study the book of Job; read *The Problem of Pain* by C. S. Lewis. |
| 4 | **How can I understand the Trinity?** | Matthew 28:19 (NIV): "Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." | The Trinity is one God in three Persons—not three gods. Each Person is fully divine, co-equal, and co-eternal. The doctrine emerges from Scripture's testimony that the Father is God (Ephesians 1:3), the Son is God (John 1:1), and the Holy Spirit is God (Acts 5:3–4), yet there is only one God (Deuteronomy 6:4). | Read *Delivering the Doctrine* by Millard Erickson; trace Trinitarian passages in John 14–17. |
| 5 | **What about predestination vs. free will?** | Ephesians 1:11 (NIV): "In him we were also chosen, having been predestined according to the plan of him who works out everything in conformity with the purpose of his will." | Scripture affirms both God's sovereign election and human responsibility. Romans 9 addresses divine choice; Romans 10 calls for belief. The two truths stand side by side without the Bible resolving the philosophical tension. Most traditions emphasize one dimension more than the other, but both are biblical. | Compare Romans 9–11 with John 6:44 and Revelation 22:17; read *Predestination and Free Will* ed. by David Basinger. |
| 6 | **How do I explain apparent contradictions in the Bible?** | Proverbs 25:2 (NIV): "It is the glory of God to conceal a matter; to search out a matter is the glory of kings." | Most alleged contradictions dissolve when context, genre, and original language are considered. Numerical differences often reflect rounding or textual variants; theological "tensions" (e.g., James 2:24 vs. Romans 3:28) address different audiences and different questions, not opposing truths. | Consult *Encyclopedia of Bible Difficulties* by Gleason Archer; study parallel accounts in the Gospels. |
| 7 | **How should I understand the book of Revelation?** | Revelation 1:1 (NIV): "The revelation from Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants what must soon take place." | Revelation is apocalyptic literature—symbolic, not literalistic. Its central message is Christ's ultimate victory over evil and the establishment of God's kingdom. Historicist, futurist, preterist, and idealist interpretations each capture part of its meaning. The safest approach: focus on what Revelation reveals about Christ rather than debates over timelines. | Read *Revelation* by Craig Keener; compare Daniel 7–12 with Revelation 13. |
| 8 | **What is the role of women in the church?** | Galatians 3:28 (NIV): "There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus." | Women are equal in spiritual standing before God. Key passages about leadership (1 Timothy 2:12; 1 Corinthians 14:34–35) are context-specific and debated among complementarians and egalitarians. Both positions affirm women's giftedness and dignity; they differ on whether certain offices are gender-differentiated. | Study Romans 16 (women co-workers of Paul); read *Women in the Church* by Andreas Köstenberger and Margaret Köstenberger. |
| 9 | **How do I choose a Bible translation?** | Nehemiah 8:8 (NIV): "They read from the Book of the Law of God, making it clear and giving the meaning so that the people understood what was being read." | Formal-equivalence translations (NASB, ESV) preserve original wording; dynamic-equivalence translations (NIV, NLT) prioritize readability. For study, use a formal-equivalence version; for daily reading, a balanced translation like the NIV serves well. Comparing two translations side by side sharpens understanding. | Review *How to Choose a Translation for All Its Worth* by Gordon Fee and Mark Strauss. |

Daily reading plans provide structure; journaling questions as they arise turns passive reading into active engagement. | Try a 30-day reading plan starting with John; pair it with a simple study guide. |

## How to Ask Better Bible Questions

Not all questions yield the same insight. The way you frame a question shapes the answer you find. Below are five practices that sharpen biblical inquiry and move you from confusion toward clarity.

**1. Ask what the text says before asking what it means.** Observation precedes interpretation. Read the passage slowly, noting who is speaking, to whom, and in what historical setting. Jesus Himself modeled this: "Have you never read in the Scriptures?" (Matthew 21:42, NIV). Begin with the plain text; interpretation follows.

**2. Ask in community, not only in isolation.** The Bible was written for communities of faith, not lone scholars. Proverbs 27:17 says, "As iron sharpens iron, so one person sharpens another" (NIV). A small group, a mentor, or a trusted pastor can expose blind spots that solitary reading cannot.

**3. Ask with humility, not with suspicion.** James 1:5 promises, "If any of you lacks wisdom, you should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault" (NIV). The posture of the questioner matters. Seeking truth from God is different from demanding proof from God—the former opens understanding; the latter closes it.

**4. Ask follow-up questions, not only first-level questions.** A first-level question might be "What does this verse mean?" A deeper question is "How does this verse connect to the argument of the whole chapter?" Still deeper: "How does this theme develop across the entire biblical narrative?" Layered questioning builds layered understanding.

**5. Ask practical questions that move toward obedience.** Knowledge without application deadens faith. After interpreting a passage, ask: "What does this require of me today?" Jesus concluded the Sermon on the Mount with a call to act: "Everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock" (Matthew 7:24, NIV). The best questions end in doing, not merely knowing.

These five practices are not merely academic habits; they are spiritual disciplines. The difference between a questioner who grows and a questioner who stagnates often comes down to method—whether one treats questions as doors to walk through or walls to stare at.

## FAQ

**Q1: Is it normal for Christians to have doubts and questions about the Bible?**
Yes. Doubt is not the opposite of faith; it is an element of faith. The American Bible Society's 2025 report shows that 56% of Americans—including practicing Christians—are curious about the Bible or Jesus. Questions indicate engagement, not abandonment. Even the apostles asked questions constantly (see Luke 24:38–39; John 14:5).

**Q2: What is the difference between a healthy question and a skeptical challenge?**
A healthy question seeks truth and remains open to Scripture's authority: "If any of you lacks wisdom, you should ask God" (James 1:5, NIV). A skeptical challenge presupposes error and demands that the Bible prove itself on terms the questioner controls. The posture distinguishes the two—curiosity versus confrontation.

**Q3: How many Christians actually read the Bible regularly?**
According to the American Bible Society's *State of the Bible 2025*, 41% of American adults are classified as Bible users (reading Scripture on their own at least three times per year). Only 20% qualify as "Scripture-engaged"—a deeper metric measuring consistent, transformative interaction. Barna data (2024) shows that only 41% of theological evangelicals read or study the Bible daily. The gap between belief and practice remains significant.

**Q4: Where should I go when a Bible question is too complex for a simple answer?**
Start with your local church: pastors, elders, and small-group leaders are trained to handle complex theological questions. Supplement with trusted commentaries (e.g., *The New International Commentary on the New Testament*), academic introductions to each book of the Bible, and online seminary-level courses from institutions like Biola, Dallas Theological Seminary, or Gordon-Conwell. For questions that bridge multiple disciplines—such as suffering, ethics, or eschatology—consider enrolling in a structured study program rather than relying on piecemeal internet searches. Community + resources + patience yield the deepest answers.
