# SOAP Bible Study Method — A Complete Guide with Examples

The SOAP Bible study method works by guiding you through four sequential steps — Scripture, Observation, Application, and Prayer — that move you from reading a passage to personally responding to God. Each step builds on the previous one, transforming passive reading into an active encounter with Scripture. Developed by Pastor Wayne Cordeiro, this framework has become one of the most widely used personal devotional practices among Christians worldwide.

---

## What Is the SOAP Bible Study Method?

SOAP is an acronym that stands for **Scripture**, **Observation**, **Application**, and **Prayer** — four steps that structure a single Bible study session from text to transformation. The method was created by **Pastor Wayne Cordeiro**, founding pastor of New Hope Christian Fellowship in Honolulu, Hawaii, and first published in his 2007 book *The Divine Mentor: Growing Your Faith as You Sit at the Feet of the Savior*. Cordeiro designed SOAP not as a replacement for deep theological study, but as a daily discipline that keeps believers connected to God's Word one passage at a time.

| Letter | Meaning | Purpose |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **S** | Scripture | Read and write out the passage |
| **O** | Observation | Notice what the text actually says |
| **A** | Application | Connect the passage to your life |
| **P** | Prayer | Respond to God in direct conversation |

The appeal of SOAP lies in its simplicity. A single session typically takes **10–30 minutes**, requires no theological training, and works with any Bible translation or reading plan. According to the American Bible Society's 2024 *State of the Bible* report, only **18 percent of American adults** — roughly 47 million people — qualify as Scripture Engaged, down from 28 percent (71 million) in 2020 (American Bible Society, 2024). A method like SOAP addresses that gap directly: it gives disengaged readers a concrete, repeatable entry point into the text rather than leaving them to wonder where to begin.

## How to Use SOAP — Step-by-Step Breakdown

Each SOAP step serves a distinct purpose and follows a clear order. Below is a detailed breakdown of all four steps, with key sub-points and Scripture references for each.

### Step 1: Scripture

The first step is the simplest: **read the passage and write it out by hand**. Writing forces you to slow down and notice every word, rather than skimming past phrases your eyes already recognize. Research by Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014) found that students who took notes by hand scored **12–20 percent higher** on conceptual understanding tests than those who typed the same material, because handwriting compels the brain to process meaning rather than merely transcribe (Mueller & Oppenheimer, *Psychological Science*, 2014).

- **Select a short passage** — 1 to 5 verses is ideal for beginners. You can follow a reading plan, revisit a sermon text, or continue through a book you are already reading.
- **Read the passage at least twice** — once for overall flow and once for detail. Reading aloud can further slow your pace and sharpen attention.
- **Write the verses out word for word** in your journal, noting the book, chapter, and translation. For example: Philippians 4:6–7 (NIV).
- **Compare translations if a phrase confuses you** — the NIV, ESV, and NLT each render Greek or Hebrew idioms differently. A quick comparison can unlock meaning.
- **Mark the date and passage reference** at the top of each journal entry so you can track your progress and revisit earlier insights.

### Step 2: Observation

Observation is the "detective work" of Bible study. Before deciding what a passage means for you, you must first determine what it actually says. This step trains you to read objectively — noticing facts, patterns, and literary features rather than jumping to personal interpretation.

- **Identify who is speaking and who is addressed** — Paul writes to the Philippians; Jesus speaks to the Pharisees; the Psalmist cries out to God. Context changes meaning.
- **Note the setting or occasion** — Paul writes Philippians from prison (Philippians 1:13–14, NIV): "It has become evident throughout the whole palace guard and to everyone else that I am in chains for Christ." That backdrop makes his command to rejoice all the more striking.
- **Look for repeated words or phrases** — repetition signals emphasis. In Philippians 4:4–7 (NIV), "rejoice" appears twice, "peace" anchors the promise, and "anything" and "every" widen the scope.
- **Identify commands, promises, warnings, or descriptions of God's character** — these are the hooks that will drive your Application step. A command tells you what to do; a promise tells you what to trust.
- **Record only what the text states** — resist the urge to interpret or apply at this stage. Write observations as factual statements: "Paul commands the reader not to be anxious about *anything* — the word leaves no exceptions."

### Step 3: Application

Application bridges the ancient text and your present life. This is where SOAP distinguishes itself from purely academic study: you are not merely learning what Paul said to Philippians in the first century; you are deciding what God is saying to *you* today.

- **Ask: How does this passage speak to my current situation?** — A verse about anxiety lands differently for someone facing a medical diagnosis than for someone choosing a career path. Let your real circumstances shape the question.
- **Look for a specific action, not a vague intention** — "I should pray more" is forgettable; "Tonight, before I open my phone, I will name one worry and give it to God in prayer" is actionable. specificity is what turns reading into obedience.
- **Identify a belief to hold, a habit to change, or a sin to confess** — James 1:22 (NIV) warns, "Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says." Application is the doing.
- **Write one concrete application per session** — trying to apply every insight dilutes focus. Choose the most pressing point and commit to it.
- **Consider how the passage reshapes your view of God, yourself, or others** — sometimes Application is not a new action but a corrected understanding. A verse may reveal that God is nearer than you assumed, or that your neighbor's struggle mirrors your own.

### Step 4: Prayer

Prayer turns your study into a conversation. Instead of ending with notes on a page, you respond directly to the God who spoke through the text. This step completes the loop: Scripture reveals truth → Observation clarifies it → Application puts it into action → Prayer submits the action to God's power.

- **Begin by thanking God for what the passage revealed** — gratitude anchors your prayer in the text rather than drifting to unrelated requests.
- **Confess what the passage exposed** — if Application identified a sin or a stubborn habit, name it honestly. 1 John 1:9 (NIV) promises, "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just and will forgive us our sins and purify us from all unrighteousness."
- **Ask for strength to carry out your application** — you wrote a concrete step; now ask God to enable it. Dependence on grace, not self-effort, is the engine of obedience.
- **Pray the passage back to God** — turn the verse's language into your own words. If the text says "present your requests to God" (Philippians 4:6, NIV), then present your requests.
- **Keep the prayer brief and honest** — SOAP prayers are not sermons. A few sincere sentences align your heart with the text far more effectively than a polished monologue.

## SOAP Method Example — Philippians 4:6-7

Below is a complete SOAP walkthrough using one of the most frequently studied passages in the New Testament. The table shows each step, its purpose, and a sample journal entry you could write.

> *"Do not be anxious about anything, but in every situation, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which transcends all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus."* — Philippians 4:6–7 (NIV)

| Step | What You Write | Example Entry |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **S — Scripture** | Copy the passage word for word, noting the reference and translation. | Philippians 4:6–7 (NIV): "Do not be anxious about anything, but in every situation, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which transcends all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus." |
| **O — Observation** | List objective facts: who, what, keywords, commands, promises. | • Paul writes from prison (Phil 1:13–14). • "Do not be anxious about anything" — no exceptions. • Three prayer types: prayer (general communion), petition (specific request), thanksgiving (gratitude before answers). • Result: not ordinary calm, but "the peace of God" that actively "guards" hearts and minds. |
| **A — Application** | Write one specific, personal, actionable response. | I have carried anxiety about a work decision for two weeks without bringing it to God. This passage commands me to present that specific request — not a vague "help me with stress" — and to include thanksgiving before I see any outcome. Tonight I will write down the decision, turn it into a named petition, and list three things I am already thankful for in this situation. |
| **P — Prayer** | Respond to God directly, using the passage's language. | Lord, I confess that I have worried about this decision more than I have prayed about it. You tell me to bring *everything* to You — even the ordinary fears I think are too small. I present this decision to You now, and I thank You for the three ways You have already provided in this season. Guard my heart and mind with Your peace tonight. In Jesus' name, Amen. |

This example demonstrates the full arc of SOAP: the text enters through Scripture, is clarified through Observation, becomes personal through Application, and is sealed through Prayer. Notice how each step naturally flows into the next — Observation supplies the raw material for Application, and Application fuels the content of Prayer.

## When SOAP Works Best — and When to Try Other Methods

SOAP excels as a **daily devotional discipline**, but it is not the only tool a believer needs. Understanding its strengths and limitations helps you choose the right method for the right moment.

| Method | Best For | Typical Time | Key Characteristic |
|--------|----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **SOAP** | Daily personal devotions, beginners, journaling | 10–30 min | Simple, repeatable, personal |
| **Inductive Bible Study** | Deep book-by-book analysis, theological study | 30–60 min | Three phases: Observation → Interpretation → Application |
| **Lectio Divina** | Meditative, contemplative reading | 15–30 min | Four movements: Read → Reflect → Respond → Rest |
| **Verse Mapping** | Visual learners, word-study enthusiasts | 20–45 min | Diagrams word origins, cross-references, themes |
| **Topical Study** | Exploring a specific theme (e.g., forgiveness) | 30–60 min | Gathers multiple passages on one subject |

### When SOAP works best

SOAP is most effective when you need a **consistent, low-barrier daily practice**. It works well for morning devotions, small-group preparation, and seasons when you want to maintain Scripture engagement without committing to a lengthy study. Its journal format also creates a personal commentary over time — flipping back through months of SOAP entries reveals patterns in how God has spoken to you.

### When other methods serve you better

If you are studying a full book of the Bible and need to trace argument structure, **Inductive Bible Study** offers deeper analytical tools. If you are in a contemplative season and want to sit with a single phrase rather than dissect it, **Lectio Divina** prioritizes listening over listing. If you are wrestling with a specific life issue, a **Topical Study** pulls together every relevant passage rather than isolating one. SOAP is the reliable daily tool; these other methods are the specialty instruments for particular tasks.

## FAQ

### 1. How long should a SOAP Bible study session take?

A single-verse SOAP session typically takes **10–15 minutes**. A longer passage or a chapter-level entry may require **20–30 minutes**. The goal is consistency, not duration. A ten-minute SOAP entry five days a week will accumulate into a substantial journal over months, whereas a sporadic one-hour session is easily abandoned.

### 2. Do I need a special journal or Bible translation for SOAP?

No. Any notebook works — the critical element is handwriting rather than typing, because writing by hand engages deeper cognitive processing (Mueller & Oppenheimer, 2014). For translation, use one you understand readily; the NIV and NLT are popular for SOAP because their language is accessible. You can compare other translations when a phrase needs clarification.

### 3. Can I use SOAP in a small group?

Yes. SOAP is well suited for group use: each member completes a personal SOAP entry on the same passage during the week, then the group gathers to share Observations and Applications. Because each person's life context differs, the same verse will yield different applications — and that diversity enriches everyone's understanding.

### 4. What if I cannot find a personal application in a passage?

Some passages — genealogies, architectural measurements, historical narratives — do not yield an obvious personal application every time. In those cases, your Application might focus on a broader truth: what the passage reveals about God's character, God's faithfulness across generations, or the stakes of obedience versus disobedience. Even a general observation about God's nature can be turned into a concrete prayer.
